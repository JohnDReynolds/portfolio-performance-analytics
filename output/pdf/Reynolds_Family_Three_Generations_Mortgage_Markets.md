# Three Generations in the American Mortgage Market

## The Reynolds family and the changing boundary between public purpose and private risk

Prepared July 28, 2026

Revised July 28, 2026 to identify John Reynolds's role at American
Underwriters and streamline the Roven-Citadel discussion.

This family history follows Leon Kirkwood "Kirk" Reynolds, his son Daniel
Kirkwood Reynolds, and his grandson John Daniel Reynolds through three distinct
eras of American mortgage finance. It asks how government intervention that
helped stabilize housing during the Great Depression later interacted with
interest-rate risk, deposit insurance, deregulation, regulatory accounting,
securitization, fraud, and financial crisis.

The report is intentionally candid about causation. Family testimony is
preserved as family testimony. Documentary evidence is used where it exists.
Interpretations are separated from established facts. The history does not
support a claim that one statute, one agency, or one company caused every later
failure. It does support a broader conclusion: public programs repeatedly solved
urgent housing-finance problems while creating new incentives, dependencies,
and expectations that became dangerous when market conditions and government
rules changed.

---

## 1. Executive summary

Three generations of the Reynolds family occupied three very different
positions in the American mortgage system:

- **Leon Kirkwood "Kirk" Reynolds (1889-1963)** worked inside the
  savings-and-loan and federal housing systems as they were being rebuilt during
  the Depression. He inspected mortgage loans, represented Oregon
  savings-and-loan associations, advocated access to the new Federal Home Loan
  Bank, became an early Federal Housing Administration underwriter, and later
  served as a Federal Home Loan Bank director and savings-and-loan president.
  Family testimony remembers his work as helping homeowners hold onto their
  homes. The documentary record shows that his policy and underwriting work was
  closely aligned with that purpose, although individual borrower files have
  not yet been found. [S01-S06]

- **Daniel Kirkwood Reynolds (1932-2025)** entered the same broad field through
  residential real-estate appraisal. A 1959 directory places him at Benj.
  Franklin Federal Savings and Loan Association. Family testimony states that
  Daniel later lost more than 80 percent of his money when federal regulators
  took control of Benj. Franklin in 1990. The precise form and amount of
  Daniel's investment have not yet been documented. The public record
  nevertheless confirms that Benj. Franklin shareholders suffered a severe
  loss: litigation described the institution's stock-market value as falling
  from approximately $35 million before the disputed government action to zero
  afterward, although the resulting damages judgment was later vacated in part.
  [S01, S07, S08, S18-S21]

- **John Daniel Reynolds**, Kirk's grandson and Daniel's son, was an analyst at
  Alfred Roven's American Underwriters in the mid-1980s. This employment and
  title are supplied by family testimony. Public records identify American
  Underwriters as a Roven-controlled securities and real-estate investment
  company. The Roven group held a large position in Citadel Holding
  Corporation, the Glendale-based parent of Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan.
  Family testimony says the investment operation profited from
  government-created distortions in the savings-and-loan market. John's exact
  assignments and trades have not yet been documented. [S01, S22, S23]

The broader financial setting was the 1980s savings-and-loan crisis:

- Thrifts held long-term, fixed-rate mortgages funded by shorter-term deposits.
- Inflation and sharply higher market interest rates made those deposits much
  more expensive than the income on old mortgages.
- Deposit-rate ceilings caused funds to leave thrifts for higher-yielding money
  market products.
- Congress then removed rate ceilings, expanded thrift powers, and increased
  federal deposit-insurance coverage.
- Weak capital rules, brokered deposits, regulatory forbearance, and
  "supervisory goodwill" allowed many economically insolvent institutions to
  remain open.
- Some managers gambled for recovery; some lacked the expertise for new
  activities; and some committed fraud or insider abuse.
- The government delayed resolving the insolvent insurance fund and weak
  institutions, increasing ultimate losses.

Official histories attribute roughly 1,300 thrift failures from 1980 through
1994 and an estimated taxpayer cost of $132 billion to this combination of
interest-rate risk, deregulation, poor lending, weak supervision, and in some
cases fraud. [S12-S17]

Benj. Franklin's case was more particular and more ironic. It had acquired
other thrifts with federal approval and counted acquisition-related
"supervisory goodwill" as regulatory capital. Congress's 1989 reform law,
FIRREA, sharply restricted that treatment. A federal appellate opinion states
that the change immediately left Benj. Franklin with a tangible-capital
deficiency of nearly $178 million. Regulators placed it into conservatorship on
February 20, 1990, and sold most of its business to Bank of America later that
year. Courts later concluded that the government had breached its agreement
concerning the Western Heritage transaction, but not that an enforceable
agreement had existed for every goodwill item claimed by the shareholders.
[S18-S21]

Thus Daniel's loss was not simply the result of ordinary bad mortgage loans, and
the sources reviewed do not establish fraud at Benj. Franklin. It arose in a
system where the government had first encouraged mergers and accepted an
accounting asset as capital, then changed the capital rules when the national
crisis made that treatment untenable. That regulatory reversal may be the
clearest example in this family history of government policy first supporting
an institution and later destroying the value on which its owners had relied.

## 2. Evidence and interpretation

This report uses four categories:

- **Documented fact:** Supported by a contemporary directory, newspaper,
  government publication, database, statute, or judicial opinion.
- **Family testimony:** Supplied by a direct descendant and retained as an
  important part of the family's historical record.
- **Reasoned interpretation:** An explanation supported by documented facts but
  not itself stated in a source.
- **Contested interpretation:** A causal conclusion on which credible official
  or scholarly accounts disagree.

The distinctions matter particularly for three assertions:

1. Kirk helped homeowners retain their homes. This is family testimony strongly
   consistent with his documented work, but individual homeowner cases have
   not yet been found.
2. Daniel lost more than 80 percent of his money in the Benj. Franklin
   takeover. This is family testimony consistent with the documented collapse
   in shareholder value, but Daniel's account statements and security holdings
   have not been located.
3. John was an analyst at Alfred Roven's American Underwriters in the
   mid-1980s, and the operation profited from government distortions in the S&L
   market. John's employment, title, and the profit characterization are family
   testimony. Public records independently document American Underwriters,
   Roven's control, and the Roven group's large Citadel position. John's
   employment records and specific analyses or trades have not yet been
   located. [S22, S23]

None of these limitations diminishes the importance of the memories. Labeling
them accurately protects the memories from being confused with later inference
and gives the family a practical list of documents to seek.

## 3. The three-generation family arc

The family history can be summarized as a movement through three roles:

### First generation: building and administering the rescue system

Kirk worked in loan inspection, institutional liquidity, underwriting, and
savings-and-loan leadership. His career coincided with the creation of the
Federal Home Loan Bank System, the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, the Federal
Housing Administration, federal thrift charters, and federal deposit insurance
for savings associations.

### Second generation: appraising property and bearing regulatory loss

Daniel worked in residential appraisal, including employment at Benj. Franklin.
He later experienced the federal resolution of that institution not as an
abstract policy event but as a devastating personal financial loss.

### Third generation: observing and trading the distortions

John entered the market from the capital-markets side as an analyst at Alfred
Roven's American Underwriters. The Roven group held a large position in
Citadel Holding, the parent of Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan in Glendale,
California. John's exact assignments remain to be recovered, but his employer
and role are now identified. [S01, S22, S23]

The family therefore saw the mortgage system as builder, casualty, and market
observer. This is an unusually coherent multigenerational window into American
financial history.

## 4. Leon Kirkwood Reynolds: mortgage finance as public recovery

### Early loan work

The earliest located professional record identifies Kirk Reynolds in 1925 as
an inspector of loans for Equitable Savings and Loan Association. He traveled
through Oregon on association business. Loan inspection required practical
judgment about property value, collateral, construction, borrower risk, and
institutional lending standards. [S02]

This was still the older savings-and-loan model: local savers supplied deposits,
and a local institution turned those funds into residential mortgages that it
usually held. The institution's knowledge of borrowers and properties was a
strength. Its dependence on local deposits and its concentration in long-term
mortgages were vulnerabilities.

### The Depression and the Home Loan Bank

In October 1932, Kirk Reynolds and Ben Hazen returned from a statewide
savings-and-loan meeting advocating operation of the new Home Loan Bank in
Portland. The stated objective was to relieve pressure on associations and
allow building activity to resume. In February 1933, Kirk was identified as
executive secretary of the Oregon association of savings-and-loan
organizations. He was monitoring legislation that would let Oregon
associations join the Home Loan Bank and borrow against mortgages already in
their portfolios. [S03, S04]

This mechanism addressed a basic crisis problem. A thrift might hold sound
home mortgages but have too little cash to honor withdrawals or make new
loans. By pledging mortgages to a regional Home Loan Bank, it could obtain an
"advance" - a secured loan that converted illiquid mortgage assets into
immediate funding.

The policy was not charity to a particular borrower. It was institutional
liquidity. Yet keeping the mortgage lender liquid could prevent a forced sale,
support refinancing, and reduce pressure to call or foreclose loans. That is
why the family memory that Kirk helped homeowners hold onto their homes is
plausible and consistent with his documented work. The strongest wording is:

> Family testimony remembers Kirk as helping homeowners retain their homes
> during the Depression. Contemporary records document his work to give Oregon
> savings-and-loan institutions access to federal liquidity and housing
> programs that were intended to stabilize mortgage lending.

### The wider federal rescue structure

Kirk's work intersected with three foundational federal interventions:

1. **Federal Home Loan Bank Act of 1932.** It created a regional system that
   advanced funds to member home-financing institutions against mortgage
   collateral. The system was designed when housing finance had largely ceased
   functioning and foreclosures were rising sharply. [S09, S10]
2. **Home Owners' Loan Act of 1933.** It created the Home Owners' Loan
   Corporation to refinance mortgages of homeowners in danger of losing their
   homes, authorized federal savings-and-loan charters, and broadened Home Loan
   Bank credit activities. [S10]
3. **National Housing Act of 1934.** It created the FHA, authorized federal
   mortgage insurance, helped replace short balloon loans with long-term
   amortizing mortgages, and established the Federal Savings and Loan Insurance
   Corporation. [S10, S11]

These programs attacked different failure points:

- The Home Loan Banks supplied liquidity to lenders.
- The Home Owners' Loan Corporation directly refinanced distressed borrowers.
- The FHA insured qualifying mortgages against lender loss.
- Federal thrift insurance protected depositors and reduced the danger of runs.

### Kirk at the FHA

In March 1935, the Oregon Statesman identified Kirk as acting chief underwriter
for the FHA's Portland office. He was coordinating an FHA real-property and
housing survey in Salem. By 1940, a Honolulu directory listed Kirk as the FHA
chief underwriter there. [S05, S06]

FHA underwriting was a gatekeeping function. Government insurance reduced a
lender's credit risk, but only if the mortgage and property met federal
requirements. The underwriter therefore sat at the boundary between public
risk protection and private lending.

Kirk's role should not be romanticized as exclusively benevolent. Early federal
housing systems also institutionalized discriminatory property standards and
redlining. FHFA's historical review acknowledges that the Home Loan Bank Board,
the Home Owners' Loan Corporation, and the FHA used residential-security maps
and practices that harmed Black neighborhoods and other excluded communities.
There is no evidence located that attributes such a decision personally to
Kirk, but his professional system had both stabilizing achievements and
profound exclusions. [S09]

### Later leadership

Kirk later became a Class C director of the Federal Home Loan Bank of San
Francisco and president of First Federal Savings and Loan Association of
Portland. His career was not a sequence of unrelated jobs. It followed the
institutional architecture of federally supported housing finance from local
loan inspection to federal underwriting, regional liquidity governance, and
thrift executive leadership.

## 5. Why the Depression-era system worked

The Depression reforms produced durable benefits because they addressed real
structural defects.

### Liquidity without immediate mortgage liquidation

Before the Home Loan Banks, a thrift facing withdrawals could not readily turn
long-term home loans into cash. Advances reduced the need to dump assets or
stop lending entirely.

### Long-term amortization

Earlier mortgages often required large down payments, short maturities, and a
balloon payment. FHA insurance helped make longer, fully amortizing mortgages
standard. The borrower could retire principal gradually instead of facing a
large refinancing event every few years. [S10, S11]

### Risk pooling

Mortgage insurance spread default losses rather than leaving one local lender
to absorb the full impact. Deposit insurance reassured savers that a thrift
failure would not erase insured deposits.

### Standardization

Federal underwriting standards made mortgages more comparable. Standardization
could lower transaction costs and attract capital beyond a single neighborhood.

### Countercyclical public capacity

The federal government could support mortgage credit when private balance
sheets and local markets were impaired. This was the public purpose Kirk helped
administer.

These were genuine achievements. The later crises do not prove that the
Depression programs were mistakes. They show that a successful emergency
architecture can become dangerous if its rules, guarantees, and incentives are
not adapted as the market changes.

## 6. How stability became structural fragility

The thrift model paired two sides of a balance sheet:

- **Assets:** long-term, often fixed-rate residential mortgages.
- **Liabilities:** deposits that could leave or reprice much sooner.

When rates were stable, the model worked. A thrift might earn 6 percent on a
long mortgage while paying 3 percent on deposits. The spread paid expenses,
absorbed losses, and generated earnings.

When market rates rose sharply, the old mortgage still earned 6 percent while
depositors demanded 8, 10, or 12 percent. The institution could lose money on
every dollar of funding. Even if every homeowner paid on time, the thrift could
be economically insolvent.

This is the central fact of the first phase of the S&L crisis. It was primarily
an interest-rate and balance-sheet crisis, not initially a wave of homeowner
defaults or fraud. A federal appellate court later summarized that short-term
deposit costs exceeded the income on long-term fixed mortgages and that more
than 400 thrifts failed between 1981 and 1983. [S18]

Government policy had helped establish the long-term fixed-rate mortgage and
the specialized thrift. Those choices made homeownership more accessible, but
they concentrated duration risk inside institutions that lacked modern hedging,
diversification, and sufficient capital.

## 7. The S&L crisis: how policy incentives became distortions

The crisis developed through interacting stages rather than a single decision.

### Stage 1: rate ceilings and disintermediation

Federal rules limited what banks and thrifts could pay depositors. When
inflation and market rates rose, money-market funds and Treasury instruments
offered more attractive returns. Depositors withdrew funds from thrifts.
Mortgage lending contracted. [S12, S13]

### Stage 2: removal of ceilings and higher funding costs

The Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 began
phasing out deposit-rate ceilings. This helped thrifts compete for money but did
not repair the low yields on their old mortgage portfolios. It also increased
federal deposit-insurance coverage from $40,000 to $100,000. [S13, S15]

### Stage 3: expanded powers

The Garn-St Germain Act of 1982 and related state and federal changes allowed
thrifts to enter commercial real estate, acquisition and development lending,
consumer loans, and other activities outside their traditional expertise.
Policymakers hoped new profits would restore solvency. Some institutions did
recover. Others took much greater risk. [S12-S15]

### Stage 4: insured hot money

Brokered deposits allowed a thrift to attract large volumes of funds from
outside its community. Deposit insurance reduced depositors' incentive to
evaluate the institution's risk. A weak thrift could therefore promise a high
rate, gather federally protected money, and make speculative investments.

This was a classic moral-hazard structure: the institution and its owners
captured much of the upside if risky investments succeeded, while the insurance
fund and taxpayers bore much of the downside if they failed.

### Stage 5: paper capital and supervisory goodwill

Regulators encouraged healthier thrifts to acquire failed institutions because
the insurance fund lacked the money to close every failure. The excess of an
acquired thrift's liabilities over the fair value of its assets could be booked
as "supervisory goodwill" and counted toward regulatory capital, then amortized
over many years.

This accounting treatment solved an immediate political and administrative
problem. It allowed mergers to occur without the acquiring institution
instantly appearing insolvent. But the goodwill was not cash and could not
absorb withdrawals or loan losses. Reported regulatory capital and tangible
economic capacity diverged.

### Stage 6: forbearance

Regulators allowed many insolvent or marginal institutions to remain open in
the hope that earnings would restore them. This delayed recognition of losses.
It also gave managers time and incentive to make increasingly risky bets - the
behavior commonly called "gambling for resurrection."

### Stage 7: fraud and insider abuse

Expanded powers, fast growth, weak controls, complex real-estate transactions,
and thin supervision created opportunities for fraud. The Department of
Justice later reported thousands of major financial-institution fraud
convictions across banks, thrifts, and credit unions. GAO reported thousands of
major investigations during the cleanup. [S16, S17]

Fraud was real, costly, and sometimes spectacular. It was not the whole crisis.
The FDIC's official history cautions that the majority of S&Ls were not
fraud-ridden; many failed because of interest-rate exposure, poor management,
unsafe lending, regional recession, or a combination. [S12]

### Stage 8: delayed cleanup

The Federal Savings and Loan Insurance Corporation became economically
insolvent, but political disagreement delayed adequate funding and closures.
The eventual cleanup under FIRREA and the Resolution Trust Corporation was
therefore larger and more disruptive than an earlier resolution would have
been.

The distortion was not simply "too much government" or "too little
government." It was a poorly aligned combination:

- public guarantees;
- private risk-taking;
- deregulated asset choices;
- weak capital;
- delayed supervision;
- inconsistent accounting;
- and an eventual abrupt rule change.

## 8. Fraud, crisis, and the need for precise language

The phrase "market distortions, fraud and financial crises" describes related
but distinct phenomena.

### A distortion is not necessarily fraud

A distortion exists when prices or behavior reflect a subsidy, guarantee,
regulatory rule, tax preference, or expectation rather than the full underlying
risk. Deposit insurance can distort funding costs without anyone lying.
Supervisory goodwill can distort reported capital even when it is booked under
rules approved by regulators.

### Unsafe risk-taking is not necessarily fraud

A thrift can make reckless loans, grow too quickly, or misunderstand
interest-rate risk without committing a crime. Such conduct may violate safety
and soundness standards but still differ from intentional deception.

### Fraud requires deception or other unlawful conduct

False appraisals, sham borrowers, self-dealing, land flips, concealed losses,
and diversion of institution funds were among the patterns prosecuted during
the crisis. The availability of insured funds and weak supervision made some
schemes larger.

### No fraud finding should be attached to Benj. Franklin without evidence

The records reviewed for this report describe Benj. Franklin's regulatory
capital shortfall, supervisory goodwill, government conservatorship, asset
sale, and shareholder litigation. They do not establish that Benj. Franklin
failed because of fraud. Its history should not be merged with notorious thrift
fraud cases merely because they occurred during the same national crisis.

## 9. Daniel Kirkwood Reynolds and Benj. Franklin

### A second generation in housing valuation

Daniel was born in Portland in 1932, the same year Congress created the Federal
Home Loan Bank System. He later earned a business degree, served in the Army,
and became a residential real-estate appraiser.

The 1959 Portland directory identifies Daniel K. Reynolds as an appraiser for
Benjamin Franklin Federal Savings and Loan Association. He later operated his
own appraisal company and developed a regional residential-sales data service.
His work belonged to the same housing-finance ecosystem as his father's, but
his professional specialty was the value of the underlying collateral. [S07,
S08]

### Benj. Franklin's growth and acquisitions

Benj. Franklin acquired Equitable Savings and Loan in 1982 and Western Heritage
Savings and Loan in 1985. These transactions occurred while federal regulators
were encouraging stronger institutions to absorb weak ones. Acquisition losses
could be recorded as supervisory goodwill and recognized as regulatory capital
over long amortization periods. [S18]

In 1986, Benj. Franklin sought approval to convert to public stock ownership.
Its shares subsequently became a market investment in addition to its role as
a local thrift. [S18, S21]

### FIRREA changed the bargain

Congress enacted the Financial Institutions Reform, Recovery, and Enforcement
Act in 1989. FIRREA abolished the old thrift regulator and insurance structure,
created the Office of Thrift Supervision and the Resolution Trust Corporation,
raised real capital standards, and sharply restricted the use of supervisory
goodwill.

According to the Federal Circuit's account, the new rule immediately made
Benj. Franklin insolvent for regulatory purposes and left it with a tangible
capital deficiency approaching $178 million. The Office of Thrift Supervision
appointed the RTC conservator on February 20, 1990. The FDIC failure database
records the institution as a February 21 failure with approximately $4.6
billion in assets and $3.2 billion in deposits. [S18, S19]

Most of the business was sold to Bank of America. Deposit customers continued
through the assuming institution, while common shareholders bore the collapse
in equity value. The BENJ security was removed from NASDAQ reporting in April
1990. [S20, S21]

### The long shareholder litigation

Former shareholders argued that the government had broken promises under which
Benj. Franklin acquired troubled thrifts and amortized supervisory goodwill.
The litigation produced mixed results:

- The Court of Federal Claims initially valued Franklin's stock at
  approximately $35 million before the breach and zero afterward.
- An award was later increased to approximately $52 million.
- The Federal Circuit vacated that award because it found no enforceable
  goodwill contract for the Equitable acquisition.
- The government did not contest that a contract existed and had been breached
  for the Western Heritage transaction.
- The matter was remanded to determine what damages, if any, arose from that
  narrower breach. [S18]

The legal detail matters. It would be inaccurate to say either that the
government was completely vindicated or that every aspect of the seizure was
judicially declared unlawful. The courts recognized a government breach in one
transaction while rejecting the shareholders' broader contract theory for
another.

### Daniel's loss

Family testimony states:

> Daniel Reynolds lost more than 80 percent of his money when the federal
> government took over Benj. Franklin Savings and Loan.

The statement should remain in the family history exactly as testimony. The
public record makes the scale plausible because it documents the destruction
of common shareholder value. It does not yet establish:

- whether Daniel owned common stock, an employee plan interest, options, debt,
  uninsured deposits, or another investment;
- the number of shares or original cost;
- whether "80 percent" referred to his Benj. Franklin position or his total
  wealth;
- whether he later received any receivership or litigation distribution; or
- the date at which the family measured the loss.

Those details could transform a powerful family memory into a precise financial
case study.

### The irony across two generations

Kirk worked in a period when government rules supplied liquidity and
credibility to the thrift system. Daniel suffered when the government withdrew
an accounting treatment that had supplied regulatory capital to a later
generation of thrifts.

The same public authority could be stabilizer in one era and destroyer of
private equity in another. That does not prove bad faith. It shows the danger
of building private ownership value on rules that the sovereign retains power
to change.

## 10. John Daniel Reynolds: analyst at American Underwriters

Family testimony identifies John Daniel Reynolds as an analyst at American
Underwriters, Alfred Roven's investment company, in the mid-1980s. The family
remembers the operation as heavily invested in Citadel in Southern California
and as profiting from government-created distortions in the savings-and-loan
market.

### The documented institutional setting

Public records confirm the surrounding business setting:

- Alfred Roven organized American Underwriters in 1980 primarily to transact
  business in securities, bonds, derivatives, and other investments. He was its
  sole shareholder and directed its trading activities. [S22]
- Roven also controlled Kenilworth Corporation, which traded securities and
  provided trading advice. [S22]
- Contemporary reporting identified American Underwriters and Kenilworth as
  Roven-controlled firms. Their group held 9.1 percent of Citadel Holding in
  April 1985 and later increased the position to 9.8 percent. [S23]
- Citadel Holding was the parent of Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan in
  Glendale, California. The family shorthand "Citadel S&L" refers to this
  holding-company and thrift complex. [S23]

The public records do not name John, so his employment and title remain family
testimony. They do independently establish that American Underwriters was a
specific Roven-controlled investment company and that the Roven group held a
substantial position in a Southern California thrift holding company.

### Why 1986 was an exceptional moment

By 1986:

- many thrifts were still economically weak after the rate shock;
- some could report regulatory solvency through goodwill and permissive
  accounting;
- deposit insurance protected funding up to a much higher statutory limit;
- brokered deposits could move rapidly toward the highest offered rate;
- new asset powers encouraged institutions to seek higher returns;
- regulators and the underfunded FSLIC could not close every insolvent thrift;
- real-estate booms and tax incentives affected collateral prices; and
- Congress had not yet enacted the final cleanup structure.

Different claims on the same institution could therefore carry very different
economic realities. An insured depositor, an uninsured creditor, a common
shareholder, a mortgage-security investor, and a taxpayer did not bear the same
risk even when exposed to the same thrift.

### What remains uncertain about John's work

The available records do not identify John's specific assignments, analyses,
compensation, or trades. Employment records, research memoranda, investor
letters, tax records, or trade confirmations could establish whether he worked
directly on the Citadel position or on other investments. The report should not
attribute a particular strategy to him without those records.

### The third-generation lesson

John's experience completes the family arc. A public rule intended to protect a
homeowner or depositor can create a predictable price difference elsewhere.
Sophisticated market participants may profit not by causing the rule, but by
understanding its consequences more quickly or accurately than others.

The family story is therefore not simply about government versus markets.
Government rules become part of the market. Once they affect funding costs,
capital definitions, guarantees, or closure decisions, investors trade the
rules as well as the mortgages.

## 11. What the three generations teach

### 1. Public intervention can be indispensable

The Depression mortgage market was broken. Liquidity support, refinancing, and
insurance helped restore it. Kirk's work belonged to a genuine recovery effort.

### 2. Every guarantee changes behavior

Deposit insurance protects savers and prevents runs, but it can also reduce
market discipline. A guarantee must therefore be paired with capital,
supervision, pricing, and prompt closure rules.

### 3. Accounting rules can become economic facts

Supervisory goodwill began as an accounting device to facilitate rescues. Once
institutions, investors, and acquisitions depended on it, changing the rule
destroyed real private value. Daniel's loss sits inside that transition.

### 4. Delayed loss recognition increases optionality for insiders and traders

When a weak institution remains open, owners can gamble for recovery, managers
can conceal or defer losses, and outside investors can trade rescue and closure
probabilities. John's analyst work at American Underwriters belongs to this
environment.

### 5. Fraud flourishes in complexity but is not the same as complexity

The S&L system created opportunities for fraud, yet many failures had no proven
criminal cause. Precise family history should distinguish policy error,
economic loss, unsafe practice, and intentional deception.

### 6. Government is both rule-maker and counterparty

Benj. Franklin's litigation arose because the government encouraged or approved
mergers under one capital treatment and later legislated another. The sovereign
can change rules in the public interest, but the change can also breach
enforceable commitments or strand private reliance.

## 12. A narrative family history

Leon Kirkwood Reynolds entered mortgage finance when it was still local,
personal, and geographically constrained. He traveled Oregon inspecting loans
for a savings and loan, looking at the properties and obligations behind the
institution's promises. When the Depression broke the flow of mortgage credit,
he moved into the institutional work of recovery. He advocated access to the
new Home Loan Bank, represented Oregon savings associations, and became an FHA
underwriter at the moment the federal government was remaking the American
home mortgage.

Family memory says that Kirk helped people keep their homes. The documents do
not yet name those families, but they show him working on the machinery built
for exactly that purpose: liquidity for mortgage lenders, new refinancing
capacity, insured mortgages, and a regulated savings-and-loan network. His
career later carried him to the board of the Federal Home Loan Bank of San
Francisco and the presidency of a Portland thrift. He became part of the
system's leadership.

Daniel grew up inside that world. He chose appraisal, the discipline that asks
whether a property's value justifies the mortgage resting on it. By 1959 he was
an appraiser at Benj. Franklin, a Portland institution with deep roots in the
same savings-and-loan community where Kirk had worked.

The system Daniel inherited had been extraordinarily successful at expanding
long-term mortgage credit. Its success also concealed a weakness. Thrifts
funded long mortgages with deposits that could reprice or depart much faster.
When inflation and interest rates surged, the old mortgage book could not earn
enough to pay the new cost of money.

Government responded first by relaxing constraints. Thrifts received broader
powers, easier access to insured funds, and accounting concessions. Regulators
encouraged mergers and allowed acquisition losses to appear as supervisory
goodwill. The policies kept institutions alive and postponed immediate public
cost. They also made the line between true capital and regulatory capital
increasingly important.

John entered the market during this suspended period as an analyst at Alfred
Roven's American Underwriters. The Roven group held a large position in
Citadel Holding, parent of Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan in Glendale.
Public records confirm American Underwriters' investment activities and the
group's roughly 9 to 9.8 percent Citadel position. John's specific assignments
and analyses remain to be recovered. [S01, S22, S23]

The setting is nevertheless much clearer than before. Institutions could be
protected as deposit-taking franchises while their common stock, subordinated
claims, mortgage assets, or future regulatory status carried very different
risks. The government's reluctance or inability to close weak institutions
immediately created time, uncertainty, and tradable outcomes.

The cleanup eventually came. FIRREA rejected much of the old regulatory
accounting. Benj. Franklin's supervisory goodwill could no longer support its
capital position. Regulators took control in February 1990. Deposits and
branches moved to Bank of America, but shareholder value collapsed.

For Daniel, the national policy transition was personal. Family testimony says
he lost more than four-fifths of his money. The courts later spent years asking
whether the government had broken the commitments that induced Benj. Franklin
to acquire weak institutions. The answer was not all or nothing: one claimed
contract failed, another breach stood, and the broad damages award was vacated.

Across the three lives, the role of government changed. For Kirk it supplied
the institutional tools of rescue. For Daniel it changed the rules and
destroyed private equity value. For John it created differences between
official protection, regulatory reporting, and market price that could be
analyzed and traded.

The history is not that government assistance inevitably becomes fraud.
Rather, public help changes incentives and creates reliance. If guarantees are
underpriced, capital is fictional, supervision is delayed, or rules change
abruptly, the rescue architecture can produce new risks. Private actors may
mismanage those risks, exploit them, commit fraud, lose fortunes, or profit
from recognizing them.

That is the Reynolds family's distinctive place in mortgage history: three
generations living through the creation, distortion, and repricing of the
American promise to make homeownership financially possible.

## 13. Detailed chronology

### 1925

- Kirk Reynolds is documented as an inspector of loans for Equitable Savings
  and Loan Association.

### July 22, 1932

- The Federal Home Loan Bank Act becomes law and creates the regional Home
  Loan Bank System.

### October 1932

- Kirk and Ben Hazen advocate making the Portland Home Loan Bank operational
  to relieve pressure on savings associations and revive building.
- Daniel Kirkwood Reynolds is an infant in Portland.

### February 1933

- Kirk is executive secretary of Oregon's savings-and-loan association and
  monitors legislation enabling state associations to join the Home Loan Bank
  and borrow against mortgages.

### June 1933

- The Home Owners' Loan Act creates a federal corporation to refinance
  homeowners threatened with losing their homes.

### June 1934

- The National Housing Act creates the FHA and FSLIC and promotes long-term
  insured mortgage lending.

### March 1935

- Kirk is acting FHA chief underwriter in Portland.

### 1940

- Kirk is FHA chief underwriter in Honolulu.

### 1953-1955

- Kirk serves as a director of the Federal Home Loan Bank of San Francisco.

### 1956

- Kirk is president of First Federal Savings and Loan Association of Portland.

### 1959

- Daniel is documented as a residential appraiser for Benj. Franklin Federal
  Savings and Loan Association.

### Late 1970s and early 1980s

- Inflation and high interest rates devastate thrift balance sheets.
- Short-term deposit costs exceed income from older fixed-rate mortgages.

### March 1980

- DIDMCA begins phasing out deposit-rate ceilings, expands thrift powers, and
  raises federal deposit insurance to $100,000.

### 1981-1983

- More than 400 thrifts fail as the initial interest-rate crisis takes hold.

### 1982

- Garn-St Germain expands thrift investment powers.
- Benj. Franklin acquires Equitable Savings and Loan.
- Supervisory goodwill becomes important to the combined institution's
  regulatory capital.

### April-July 1985

- Benj. Franklin acquires Western Heritage Savings and Loan with federal
  assistance and agreed regulatory forbearances.
- Alfred Roven and the companies he controls, American Underwriters and
  Kenilworth Corporation, report a 9.1 percent position in Citadel Holding,
  parent of Fidelity Federal Savings and Loan in Glendale, California.
- The Roven-led group increases its position to 9.8 percent.

### 1986

- Benj. Franklin proceeds toward public stock ownership.
- Family testimony identifies John Daniel Reynolds as an analyst at American
  Underwriters. The family remembers the Roven investment operation as heavily
  invested in Citadel and profiting from government distortions in the S&L
  market.
- Public records confirm American Underwriters' investment activities and the
  Roven group's large Citadel position, although they do not identify John.

### 1989

- FIRREA restructures thrift regulation, creates the RTC, and sharply limits
  supervisory goodwill as regulatory capital.

### February 20-21, 1990

- OTS appoints the RTC as conservator for Benj. Franklin.
- The institution is recorded as a failure with approximately $4.6 billion in
  assets and $3.2 billion in deposits.

### April 1990

- The BENJ stock symbol is removed from NASDAQ reporting.

### September 1990

- Most of Benj. Franklin's operations are sold to Bank of America.
- Family testimony states that Daniel loses more than 80 percent of his money
  as a result of the takeover.

### 1990s-2010s

- Benj. Franklin shareholders pursue long-running litigation over supervisory
  goodwill, government contracts, receivership value, and damages.

## 16. Overall conclusion

The Reynolds family did not merely work in the same industry for three
generations. Each generation encountered a different stage in the transformation
of American mortgage finance.

Kirk helped administer a public recovery architecture built when private
mortgage markets and local thrift liquidity had failed. Daniel became a
housing-value professional and then suffered a profound loss when federal
capital rules and receivership powers overtook the institution with which he
was connected. John worked as an analyst at Alfred Roven's American
Underwriters while the Roven group held a large position in Citadel Holding.
He encountered a market in which deposit guarantees, accounting conventions,
regulatory approvals, and delayed closures could create prices that did not
correspond neatly to underlying economic risk.

The history supports neither a celebration of all government housing policy nor
a claim that all intervention inevitably leads to fraud. It shows a recurring
cycle:

1. A private market fails or stops serving a public need.
2. Government supplies liquidity, insurance, standardization, or guarantees.
3. Private actors reorganize their behavior around the new rules.
4. The protection becomes a source of dependence or moral hazard.
5. Market conditions expose risks the original policy did not anticipate.
6. Regulators delay, relax rules, or change accounting to avoid immediate
   losses.
7. Some institutions recover; some fail through bad economics; some exploit
   the rules; and some commit fraud.
8. Government eventually changes the rules and reallocates the losses.

The recurring problem was the interaction of public protection with private
incentives. Deposit insurance, permissive capital rules, regulatory
forbearance, and delayed closure could preserve confidence while weakening
market discipline. When combined with interest-rate mismatch, leverage, poor
lending, or fraud, those protections could shift losses onto deposit insurers,
investors, and taxpayers.

For the Reynolds family, that national history was lived at human scale: homes
preserved, property values assessed, savings lost, and distortions recognized
as investment opportunities.
